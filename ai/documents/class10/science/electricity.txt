TOPIC: Electric Current

Electric current is the flow of electric charges through a conductor.

In metallic conductors, electric current is produced by the flow of
electrons.

A continuous and closed path through which electric current flows is
called an electric circuit.

Conventionally, the direction of electric current is taken as opposite
to the direction of flow of electrons.

Formula:

I = Q/t

Where:

I = Electric current

Q = Electric charge

t = Time

SI Unit:

Ampere (A)

1 A = 1 C/s

Small units:

1 mA = 10⁻³ A

1 μA = 10⁻⁶ A

An instrument used to measure electric current is called an
ammeter.

An ammeter is always connected in series in a circuit.  

---

TOPIC: Electric Circuit

An electric circuit is a continuous and closed path through which
electric current flows.

A basic electric circuit may contain:

1. Cell or battery
2. Connecting wires
3. Switch or plug key
4. Electrical component such as a bulb or resistor

If the circuit is broken, current stops flowing.

A switch is used to complete or break an electric circuit. 

---

TOPIC: Electric Charge

Electric charge is a property of matter responsible for electrical
effects.

The SI unit of electric charge is coulomb (C).

One coulomb of charge contains approximately:

6 × 10¹⁸ electrons

Charge on one electron:

1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Example:

If:

I = 0.5 A

t = 10 minutes = 600 s

Then:

Q = It

Q = 0.5 × 600

Q = 300 C  

---

TOPIC: Electric Potential Difference

Electric charges flow through a conductor when there is a difference
in electric potential between two points.

A cell or battery produces this potential difference.

Potential difference is defined as the work done in moving a unit
charge from one point to another.

Formula:

V = W/Q

Where:

V = Potential difference

W = Work done

Q = Charge

SI Unit:

Volt (V)

1 V = 1 J/C

One volt is the potential difference when 1 joule of work is done
to move 1 coulomb of charge.

A voltmeter is used to measure potential difference.

A voltmeter is always connected in parallel. 

---

TOPIC: Work Done in an Electric Circuit

From the formula:

V = W/Q

Therefore:

W = VQ

Where:

W = Work done in joule

V = Potential difference in volt

Q = Charge in coulomb

Example:

V = 12 V

Q = 2 C

W = VQ

W = 12 × 2

W = 24 J

TOPIC: Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that the potential difference across the ends of a
metallic conductor is directly proportional to the current flowing
through it, provided its temperature remains constant.

Therefore:

V ∝ I

V/I = Constant

The constant is called resistance.

Formula:

V = IR

Where:

V = Potential difference

I = Current

R = Resistance

The V-I graph for a conductor obeying Ohm's law is a straight line
passing through the origin. 

---

TOPIC: Resistance

Resistance is the property of a conductor by which it opposes or
resists the flow of electric charges.

Formula:

R = V/I

Also:

I = V/R

SI Unit:

Ohm (Ω)

One ohm is the resistance of a conductor when a potential difference
of 1 V produces a current of 1 A.

Therefore:

1 Ω = 1 V/A

For a constant potential difference:

Current is inversely proportional to resistance.

If resistance increases, current decreases. 

---

TOPIC: Rheostat

A rheostat is a device used to change or regulate the resistance in
an electric circuit.

It can be used to increase or decrease the current without changing
the voltage source.

A rheostat is also called a variable resistance. 

---

TOPIC: Factors Affecting Resistance

The resistance of a conductor depends on:

1. Length of the conductor
2. Area of cross-section of the conductor
3. Nature or material of the conductor
4. Temperature

Resistance is directly proportional to length:

R ∝ l

Resistance is inversely proportional to area of cross-section:

R ∝ 1/A

Combining both:

R ∝ l/A

Therefore:

R = ρl/A

Where:

R = Resistance

ρ = Resistivity of the material

l = Length of the conductor

A = Area of cross-section

A longer wire has greater resistance.

A thicker wire has lower resistance than a thinner wire of the same
material and length. 

---

TOPIC: Resistivity

Resistivity is a characteristic property of a material that determines
how strongly the material opposes the flow of electric current.

Formula:

ρ = RA/l

SI Unit:

Ohm metre (Ω m)

Resistivity depends on:

1. Nature of the material
2. Temperature

It does not depend on the length or thickness of a particular sample
of the material.

Metals generally have low resistivity and are good conductors.

Insulators such as rubber and glass have very high resistivity. 

---

TOPIC: Uses of Different Materials in Electrical Devices

Alloys generally have higher resistivity than their constituent metals.

They also do not oxidise easily at high temperatures.

Therefore, alloys are used in heating devices such as:

1. Electric irons
2. Toasters
3. Electric heaters

Tungsten is used in electric bulb filaments because it can withstand
very high temperatures.

Copper and aluminium are used for electrical transmission lines
because they have low resistivity and are good conductors.

TOPIC: Resistance in Series

When resistors are connected end to end, they are said to be
connected in series.

In a series circuit:

The same current flows through every resistor.

The total potential difference is equal to the sum of the potential
differences across individual resistors.

Formula:

V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃

The equivalent resistance is:

Rₛ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃

For more resistors:

Rₛ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

The equivalent resistance in a series combination is greater than any
individual resistance.   

---

TOPIC: Example of Resistors in Series

Given:

R₁ = 20 Ω

R₂ = 4 Ω

V = 6 V

Total resistance:

Rₛ = R₁ + R₂

Rₛ = 20 + 4

Rₛ = 24 Ω

Current:

I = V/Rₛ

I = 6/24

I = 0.25 A

Potential difference across the first resistor:

V₁ = IR₁

V₁ = 0.25 × 20

V₁ = 5 V

Potential difference across the second resistor:

V₂ = IR₂

V₂ = 0.25 × 4

V₂ = 1 V

Therefore:

V = V₁ + V₂

6 V = 5 V + 1 V  

---

TOPIC: Resistance in Parallel

When resistors are connected between the same two points, they are
said to be connected in parallel.

In a parallel circuit:

The potential difference across every resistor is the same.

The total current is equal to the sum of currents through the
individual branches.

Formula:

I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃

The equivalent resistance is given by:

1/Rₚ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

For more resistors:

1/Rₚ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

The equivalent resistance of a parallel combination is less than the
smallest individual resistance.  

---

TOPIC: Example of Resistors in Parallel

Given:

R₁ = 5 Ω

R₂ = 10 Ω

R₃ = 30 Ω

V = 12 V

Current through R₁:

I₁ = V/R₁

I₁ = 12/5

I₁ = 2.4 A

Current through R₂:

I₂ = 12/10

I₂ = 1.2 A

Current through R₃:

I₃ = 12/30

I₃ = 0.4 A

Total current:

I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃

I = 2.4 + 1.2 + 0.4

I = 4 A

Equivalent resistance:

1/Rₚ = 1/5 + 1/10 + 1/30

Rₚ = 3 Ω  

---

TOPIC: Series and Parallel Circuits

In a series circuit:

1. Current is the same throughout the circuit.
2. If one component fails, the complete circuit breaks.
3. Different appliances cannot operate independently.

In a parallel circuit:

1. The potential difference across each appliance is the same.
2. Current divides among different branches.
3. Appliances can operate independently.
4. Failure of one appliance does not stop the others.
5. Different appliances can receive the current they require.

Therefore, domestic electrical circuits use parallel connections.

TOPIC: Heating Effect of Electric Current

When electric current flows through a resistor, electrical energy may be
converted into heat.

This phenomenon is called the heating effect of electric current.

In a purely resistive circuit, electrical energy is dissipated completely
as heat.

The heating effect is used in devices such as:

1. Electric heater
2. Electric iron
3. Electric toaster
4. Electric kettle
5. Electric oven

Electrical power supplied to a circuit is:

P = VI

Energy supplied in time t is:

W = Pt

Therefore:

W = VIt

The heat produced in a resistor is:

H = VIt 

---

TOPIC: Joule's Law of Heating

According to Joule's law of heating:

H = I²Rt

Where:

H = Heat produced

I = Current

R = Resistance

t = Time

Heat produced is:

1. Directly proportional to the square of current.
2. Directly proportional to resistance.
3. Directly proportional to the time for which current flows.

Important formulas:

H = VIt

H = I²Rt

Using Ohm's law, another form is:

H = V²t/R 

---

TOPIC: Applications of Heating Effect

The heating effect of electric current is used in:

1. Electric iron
2. Electric toaster
3. Electric oven
4. Electric kettle
5. Electric heater
6. Electric bulb
7. Electric fuse

In an electric bulb, the filament becomes extremely hot and produces
light.

Tungsten is used for the filament because it has a very high melting
point.

Bulbs are generally filled with inactive gases such as nitrogen and
argon to increase the life of the filament.

TOPIC: Electric Fuse

A fuse is a safety device that protects electrical circuits and appliances
from excessively high current.

A fuse is connected in series with the circuit.

It contains a wire made of a material or alloy with an appropriate
melting point.

When excessive current flows:

1. The fuse wire becomes hot.
2. The wire melts.
3. The circuit breaks.
4. The appliance is protected.

Common domestic fuse ratings include:

1 A

2 A

3 A

5 A

10 A

Example:

For an electric iron of power 1000 W operating at 220 V:

I = P/V

I = 1000/220

I = 4.54 A

Therefore, a 5 A fuse is suitable.

TOPIC: Electric Power

Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or
dissipated.

Formula:

P = VI

Using Ohm's law:

P = I²R

Also:

P = V²/R

Where:

P = Electric power

V = Potential difference

I = Current

R = Resistance

SI Unit:

Watt (W)

1 W = 1 V × 1 A

One watt is the power consumed when a current of 1 A flows at a
potential difference of 1 V.

Larger unit:

1 kW = 1000 W 

---

TOPIC: Electrical Energy

Electrical energy is calculated as:

Electrical Energy = Power × Time

Formula:

E = Pt

The SI unit of energy is joule (J).

The commercial unit of electrical energy is kilowatt-hour (kWh).

One kilowatt-hour is commonly called one unit of electricity.

Conversion:

1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

Example:

If a refrigerator of power 400 W operates for 8 hours per day for
30 days:

Energy = Power × Time

Energy = 400 × 8 × 30

Energy = 96000 Wh

Energy = 96 kWh

If electricity costs Rs 3 per kWh:

Cost = 96 × 3

Cost = Rs 288
